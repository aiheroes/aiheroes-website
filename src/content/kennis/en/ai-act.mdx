## Does the AI Act apply to your organisation?

Almost always. The AI Act ([Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) has two main roles:

- **Provider:** whoever develops an AI system, or has one developed, and places it on the market or puts it into service under their own name.
- **Deployer:** whoever uses an AI system in their work. An organisation whose staff use ChatGPT, Copilot or an AI feature in their CRM is a deployer.

If you have your own AI application built and use it under your own name, you can be a provider yourself. That decides which obligations sit with you, and it is one of the first things we record in a build.

## Which risk class does your application fall into?

The law has four risk levels. Most applications in an ordinary organisation fall into the lowest two.

| Class | What falls under it | What you have to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Prohibited** | Social scoring, emotion recognition at work or in education, manipulation that harms people | Do not use it |
| **High risk** | AI in recruitment and selection, credit scoring, access to education or essential services, safety components of products | Risk management, logging, human oversight, documentation, registration |
| **Limited risk** | Chatbots, AI voices, generated text, images and audio | Show that it is AI |
| **Minimal risk** | Summarising, searching documents, spam filters, most internal tools | No extra requirements; AI literacy still applies |

An example: an AI assistant that answers customer questions is limited risk. It has to say that it is AI. A system that preselects job applicants is high risk, even if a recruiter has the final say.

## What applies when?

The timeline changed in 2026. With the Digital Omnibus ([Regulation (EU) 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=OJ:L_202601744), in force since 27 July 2026) the rules for high-risk AI were postponed. This is the position in October 2026:

| Date | What applies | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| 1 August 2024 | The AI Act enters into force | The clock starts |
| 2 February 2025 | Prohibited practices and AI literacy (Article 4) | Do not use prohibited practices; teach your people to work with AI |
| 2 August 2025 | Rules for general-purpose AI models, supervision and fines | Mainly for the makers of large models |
| 2 August 2026 | Transparency rules (Article 50) and most remaining provisions | Chatbots and generated content show that they are AI |
| 2 December 2026 | Marking of generated content for systems already on the market before 2 August 2026 | The transition period for existing systems ends |
| 2 December 2027 | High-risk rules for the applications in Annex III | Recruitment, credit, education and similar applications must meet every requirement |
| 2 August 2028 | High-risk rules for AI in regulated products (Annex I) | Medical devices, machinery and similar products |

The Omnibus also reworded Article 4. Organisations must take measures that support the AI literacy of their people; the law no longer demands a guaranteed level per person. The obligation itself remains. Read more in [AI literacy](/en/insights/ai-literacy).

We keep this timeline up to date and check it against the official text every quarter. The date at the top shows when that last happened.

## What to arrange now, in four steps

1. **Take stock.** List all the AI you use: standalone tools, AI features in existing software and anything you have had built. Done when: every application has an owner.
2. **Classify.** Decide the risk class of each application and whether you are the deployer or the provider. Done when: you know which applications carry extra requirements.
3. **Take care of AI literacy.** Make sure everyone who works with AI knows what it can do, where it goes wrong and what does not belong in a prompt, to the level their role needs. Done when: you can show who was trained on what, and when. See [AI literacy](/en/insights/ai-literacy).
4. **Put agreements in writing.** Which tools are allowed, which data may go into them, who oversees them and how you show that something is AI. Done when: there is a short AI policy with an owner. See [writing an AI policy](/en/insights/ai-policy).

If you use high-risk AI, human oversight and logging come on top. How you build those into a system is explained in [human oversight and logging](/en/insights/human-oversight).

## If you have AI built for you

Classification, logging and human oversight are design choices. Adding them afterwards costs more than getting them right from the start. That is why, in every build, we decide the risk class during the start sprint and record who the provider is. Logging, hand-off to a person and transparency are in the system from the first version.

One example is the assistant on this site. It says that it is AI, and [the transparency page](/en/legal/ai-assistant) explains how it works and what happens to conversations.
