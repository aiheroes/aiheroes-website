## What is different about AI security?

An AI application is software, so everything you already do for security still applies. AI adds a few risks of its own:

- **Data leaking through prompts.** Staff paste contracts, customer files or source code into an AI tool. Depending on the terms, that information may stay with the provider.
- **Prompt injection.** A model reads text from outside, such as an email or web page, and carries out the instructions hidden in it. An assistant that summarises email can be tricked into forwarding information this way. It tops the [OWASP list for applications built on language models](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/).
- **Agents with too many rights.** An agent that operates systems itself can do everything its account can do. A mistake or manipulation then has immediate consequences.
- **Dependence on one provider.** If the provider changes its model, terms or price, your application changes with it.
- **Hosting and jurisdiction.** Where the model runs and where the data sits decides which law applies.

## What to do about each risk

| Risk | Measure | Who arranges it |
|---|---|---|
| Data through prompts | Business tools with agreements, data classes, training | IT and compliance, through the AI policy |
| Prompt injection | Keep outside input apart from instructions, check output, have sensitive actions confirmed | Developers |
| Agents with too many rights | Least privilege, a person approves sensitive actions, log everything | Developers and operations |
| One provider | A model-agnostic build, documentation, an exit plan | Architect and procurement |
| Hosting and jurisdiction | Choose deliberately where the model and the data sit, data processing agreements | IT, privacy and procurement |

## AI within ISO 27001

AI applications belong in your existing information security. What you add to your management system:

1. **Inventory:** AI applications and AI features in existing software in your asset register.
2. **Risk assessment:** the risks above, per application.
3. **Supplier management:** the terms of model providers, where they store data and whether they use it for training.
4. **Logging and monitoring:** what the application does, who uses it and what goes wrong.
5. **Testing:** on top of your usual tests, tests aimed at prompt injection and misuse.

If your organisation falls under NIS2, your AI applications fall under the same duty of care as your other systems. In the Netherlands NIS2 is implemented in the [Cyberbeveiligingswet](https://www.rijksoverheid.nl/actueel/nieuws/2026/08/15/cyberbeveiligingswet-en-wet-weerbaarheid-kritieke-entiteiten-vanaf-vandaag-van-kracht) (Cybersecurity Act), in force since 15 August 2026.

## Hosting and jurisdiction

Three questions decide much of your risk: where the model runs, where the data sits, and which law the provider falls under. The US CLOUD Act can oblige American companies to hand over data they control, even when it is stored in a European data centre. So for sensitive data, choose deliberately: a European provider, or your own servers. Read more in [AI on your own servers](/en/ai-on-your-own-servers) and [software you control](/en/self-hosted).
