## What does Article 4 say?

Article 4 is a short article with a wide reach. It asks providers and deployers of AI systems to take measures for the AI literacy of their staff, and of others who work with AI on their behalf. In doing so, you take into account their knowledge, experience and education, the context in which the AI is used and the people the AI affects.

The article has applied since 2 February 2025. The Digital Omnibus ([Regulation (EU) 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=OJ:L_202601744)) amended the wording as of 27 July 2026. Organisations must now take measures that support the development of AI literacy, and the law adds that you do not have to guarantee a particular level per person. The obligation to act on it remains.

## Who does it apply to?

Everyone who works with AI or makes decisions about it. That includes organisations that only use ChatGPT, Copilot or an AI feature in existing software. What counts as "enough" differs per role:

| Role | What they need to know |
|---|---|
| **Management** | Where AI sits in the organisation, which risks that brings, which role the organisation has under the AI Act |
| **Staff who use AI** | What the tool is good and bad at, what may and may not go into a prompt, how to check an answer |
| **IT and developers** | How the system works, where the data goes, logging, oversight and security |
| **HR and procurement** | Which AI is high risk (such as AI in recruitment), what to ask of a supplier |

## What is "enough" in practice?

Role-based and demonstrable. A workable approach in five steps:

1. **Inventory:** which AI is used, and by whom.
2. **Level per role:** what each group needs to know, based on the table above.
3. **Training:** basic knowledge for everyone, more depth for those who set up, manage or decide.
4. **Records:** who was trained on what and when, and which agreements apply.
5. **Repetition:** for new tools, new roles and new people.

## How do you show it?

With your own records: who took part, on which topics, on which date, and which agreements are in your [AI policy](/en/insights/ai-policy). In its questions and answers on Article 4, the European Commission writes that external certification is not needed and that organisations record their measures themselves.

The certificate we hand out after our training is a practical tool for that. It shows what each participant covered.

## What we see most often in trainings

In the trainings we have given since 2019, to more than 1,000 professionals at organisations including the Dutch House of Representatives, the Nationale Postcode Loterij, Envalior, Hanze University of Applied Sciences, Banijay and Prosus, the same questions keep coming back:

- **What may go in?** People often do not know whether customer data, contracts or HR information may go into an AI tool. A simple split into data classes helps straight away.
- **Is the answer right?** An AI answer sounds convincing, even when it is wrong. Checking it is part of the job, just as you would review a colleague's draft.
- **Is this real?** A demonstration with a generated video or voice shows at once why generated content deserves a critical look.
- **Who is responsible?** The person who uses the result. That realisation changes how people handle AI.

## Arrange it yourself or have your team trained?

Both work. If you have your own learning department and use only a few different tools, you can set up a basic programme yourself. A training is faster when you want to reach many people in a short time, when tools with sensitive data are in use, or when you want records that are in order straight away.
