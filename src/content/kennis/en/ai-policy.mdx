## Why you need one

Your staff already use AI, with or without agreements. An AI policy makes that use visible and safe. It keeps customer data out of the wrong tool, it gives people clarity about what is allowed, and it is your evidence towards customers, auditors and regulators.

The AI Act does not require a policy document by name. But Article 4 asks you to take measures for the AI literacy of your people, and the transparency rules ask you to show when something was made or answered by AI. Agreements on paper are the simplest way to demonstrate that. Read more in [the AI Act](/en/insights/ai-act).

## What goes into an AI policy

Eight fixed sections. Together they fit on two pages.

| Section | What you record |
|---|---|
| **Purpose and scope** | Who it applies to and which kinds of AI it covers |
| **Approved tools** | Which tools are allowed, in which version (business or consumer), and how to request a new tool |
| **Data classes** | Which data may go in: public, internal, confidential, personal data |
| **Human review** | That a person checks every output before it goes out or leads to a decision |
| **Transparency** | When you tell customers they are dealing with AI |
| **Training** | Who gets which training and how you record it (Article 4) |
| **Owner and reporting** | Who manages the policy and where you report a mistake or incident |
| **Review** | When the policy is looked at again |

## Step-by-step plan

1. **Take stock of the use.** Ask teams which AI they use and for what. Done when: you have a list of tools, users and kinds of data.
2. **Classify your data.** Decide which classes of data you have and which may go into which tool. Done when: every member of staff can say in one sentence what may and may not go into a prompt.
3. **Choose your tools.** For each use, choose a tool with business terms, and a data processing agreement where personal data is involved. Done when: there is a short list of approved tools.
4. **Write the draft.** Use the eight sections above. Done when: it fits on two pages.
5. **Test it with users.** Have a few people who use AI every day read it. Done when: their questions are answered in the text.
6. **Train and record.** Make sure everyone knows it and knows how to work with it. Done when: you can show who was taken through it, and when.
7. **Plan the review.** Put a date in the calendar. Done when: there is an owner and a date.

## What often goes wrong

- **Too long.** Nobody reads twenty pages of legal text.
- **Only legal.** A policy that only forbids gets worked around. Also record what is allowed and which tool to use for it.
- **Not tested.** Without input from users it misses the situations that actually happen.
- **No owner.** Without an owner it is out of date within six months.

## If you have AI built for you

Your own AI application calls for extra agreements: what the system logs, who oversees it, where it runs and how long data is kept. In a build that is part of the design, and the agreements go into your policy. Read more in [human oversight and logging](/en/insights/human-oversight).
